Intelligence Digest

▸ Markets and industry, explained in plain language

Working analysis

What the serious money is actually arguing about

Most market commentary tells you what moved. This tries to explain why, and what usually happens next. Five big forces are pulling at each other right now — how the artificial intelligence build-out is being paid for, what is happening under the surface of the US bond market, why the oil price is misleading, the strange gap between India's policy speed and its stock market, and where money is quietly being made while attention goes somewhere else.

01

Start here: the argument about AI has changed

For two years people argued about whether AI works. That argument is basically over. The new argument is about money — specifically, who borrowed it and where the debt is written down.

Building an AI data centre is one of the most expensive things a company can do. A single large facility can cost several billion dollars before a single chip is installed. Companies like Microsoft, Meta and Alphabet are building dozens of them at once. The obvious question is: where is all that money coming from? The answer turns out to be more interesting than "profits."

Roughly $3 trillion of AI-related borrowing does not appear on the balance sheets of the companies benefiting from it — and that figure has been growing by well over a trillion dollars a year. A balance sheet is simply the list of what a company owns and what it owes. If a debt is not on that list, an investor reading the accounts will not see it. Two methods explain most of this, and both are perfectly legal, which is exactly why they are worth understanding rather than getting angry about.

Method one: build it through a separate company

When a big technology company wants a new data centre, it often does not build it directly. Instead, a separate legal company is created — usually together with a private lender — and that separate company borrows the money and builds the facility. The technology company then rents the finished building.

Under accounting rules, the parent has to combine that separate company's debt into its own accounts only if it "controls" the important decisions of that separate company. That word "control" is defined narrowly. One of the reasons Meta has given for not combining is simply that it does not control negotiations with future tenants of the building. That single limitation is enough. The debt stays outside the accounts entirely until the rental agreement actually starts.

Method two: change how long you expect the building to last

This one is subtler and, once you see it, harder to unsee.

When a company rents something, accountants split the rental into two types. If the rental period covers 75% or more of the asset's expected working life, it is treated like buying the thing with borrowed money — that is a finance lease, and it shows up as capital spending, which reduces reported cash flow. If the rental covers less than 75%, it is treated as ordinary renting — an operating lease — and it often does not.

Microsoft recently extended how long it assumes a data centre lasts, from fifteen years to twenty-five. Now do the arithmetic. A typical new data centre is rented for about fifteen years. Against a fifteen-year assumed life, that is 100% of the life, comfortably over the 75% line, so it counts as a finance lease. Against a twenty-five-year assumed life, the same fifteen-year rental is only 60% — under the line — so the identical agreement is now an operating lease.

Because Microsoft reports its free cash flow including finance-lease spending but excluding operating leases, capital spending now looks lower and free cash flow looks higher. Nothing about the actual obligation changed. The company still owes the same money for the same building. Only the presentation moved.

What this means

Neither move is fraud, and neither was hidden — both were properly disclosed, which is how anyone noticed. The signal is not about honesty. It is about timing. Companies reach for accounting flexibility when the straightforward presentation has become uncomfortable to publish. That tells you something about where we are in the cycle. It does not tell you the cycle is ending, but it does tell you the easy part is over.

The practical takeaway for anyone reading company accounts: when a business changes an assumption about how long its assets last, always ask what else that assumption touches. It is rarely just about wear and tear.

The lenders noticed before the shareholders did

There is a simple way to check how nervous professional lenders are about a company. It is called a credit default swap, and it is just an insurance contract — you pay a small amount each year, and if the company fails to repay its debts, you get paid out. The higher the price of that insurance, the more worried lenders are.

For Broadcom and Nvidia, the price of that insurance kept setting new record highs, day after day. Broadcom was simultaneously arranging up to $100 billion of financing through one of those separate-company structures. Goldman Sachs summed up the disconnect in one line: credit is asking questions that equity has largely ignored.

The pattern to hold on to here is general and it repeats across cycles. Bond investors get paid a fixed amount if things go well and lose heavily if things go badly, so they spend their time thinking about what could go wrong. Shareholders get unlimited upside, so they spend their time thinking about what could go right. When the two groups disagree sharply, the bond market has historically been the one to watch.

That last number deserves explaining because it is genuinely unusual. Correlation measures whether two things move together. Plus one means they move in lockstep. Zero means no relationship. Minus one means they move in exactly opposite directions.

The relationship between AI shares and everything else in the US market fell to minus 0.60 — the lowest ever recorded, and only the second time it has been negative at all. A few months earlier it was plus 0.65, meaning they moved together closely.

A market where AI shares rise when everything else falls, and vice versa, is not a market forming a view about the economy. It is one pool of money moving back and forth between two buckets. That arrangement tends to be unstable, because the buyer of one thing is the seller of the other, and both directions depend on the same people changing their minds.

02

Three physical limits that money cannot fix quickly

Financing is one constraint. Underneath it sit three others that are harder to argue with — equipment that is being ordered twice, electricity that does not exist, and chips that are getting more expensive at exactly the wrong moment.

Limit one: more than half the equipment orders are duplicates

A survey of fifty people who actually sign purchase orders for data centres — around 40% at the biggest cloud companies, the rest at rental data centre operators and enterprises — found that more than half of them are double-ordering.

Double-ordering means placing the same order with two or three different suppliers at once, planning to cancel the losers when one of them actually delivers. It is a completely rational response to long waiting times. If you desperately need a transformer and the wait is eighteen months, you order from three companies and take whichever arrives first. Every supply crunch in industrial history has produced this behaviour. What is unusual here is that someone measured it.

The categories where it is happening most are the ones under the greatest strain: cooling distribution units, chillers, computer room air handlers, cold plates, and medium-voltage transformers — all the unglamorous equipment that moves heat out and power in.

Why this matters in India

A group of Indian companies is currently being valued on data centre order books — cable makers, industrial pipe manufacturers, heat exchanger companies, electronics assemblers. Those order books are being presented as several years of guaranteed future revenue, and share prices reflect that certainty.

But if more than half of Western buyers are ordering the same equipment two or three times over, then part of that guaranteed revenue is a duplicate that will quietly disappear. Worse, it will disappear as a delay rather than an outright cancellation, which is much harder to spot in a quarterly result. A company can report "customer has pushed the schedule" for several quarters before anyone realises the order was never real.

The useful question to ask any company riding this theme is therefore not "how big is your order book." It is: what portion is a firm purchase order versus a letter of intent, and are you the sole supplier or one of three?

Limit two: the electricity has genuinely run out

This is the most concrete constraint in the entire picture, and it has now been confirmed by the grid operators themselves rather than by analysts.

PJM is the largest electricity grid in the United States, covering thirteen states and roughly 65 million people. Every year it works out whether it will have enough generating capacity to meet peak demand with a safety margin. For the delivery year beginning June 2028, PJM has come up 6.8 gigawatts short of its own reliability requirement. It is the first time in the grid's history that the entire system has fallen short.

PJM's response is to ask the federal regulator for permission to run a special "backstop" purchase of capacity, designed so that data centres bear the cost of the gap they created. Independent work across six major US wholesale markets reaches the same conclusion by a different route: roughly 3 gigawatts of spare room in 2026, then negative numbers in 2027 and sharply negative by 2028.

Set that against demand. AI chip electricity use is projected to reach around 315 gigawatts globally by 2033, with the United States accounting for roughly 200 of those. For scale, 1 gigawatt is roughly the output of one large nuclear reactor and enough to power something like 750,000 homes.

There is a second, less discussed engineering problem inside this. When an AI model is being trained, hundreds of thousands of chips switch on and off together in coordinated bursts. Site electricity draw can spike as much as 50% above what the facility was designed for. Those swings wear out batteries, generators and cooling equipment faster than steady use would — a maintenance and replacement cost that appears nowhere in the standard financial model for these projects.

Worth knowing

You will often see spare capacity figures that look much healthier. They usually include something called demand response, where a large user agrees in advance to cut its own consumption when the grid is stressed, in exchange for a lower tariff. On paper that counts as capacity.

The problem is that a model training run is not a factory shift you can pause. Interrupting it can mean losing days of work. So counting data centres as interruptible load is technically permitted and practically doubtful. Whenever you see a comfortable-looking grid number, check whether demand response is included.

Limit three: the chips are getting more expensive at the worst moment

Memory has become the bottleneck. Not the fancy processing chips — the ordinary memory that stores data while the processor works on it. AI systems need enormous amounts of it, and the industry did not build enough capacity.

Prices have gone vertical. Some memory chips reached record prices, and server memory costs have been widely expected to roughly double. That flowed straight through: Nvidia informed its largest customers that AI server prices would rise more than 15% on systems shipping early next year.

The winners are the memory makers. One Chinese manufacturer, YMTC, reported around 47 billion yuan of revenue with 96% of it from NAND flash memory, producing 33 billion yuan of profit — a 77% margin. This is a company that was losing money as recently as 2023. It is now raising 33 billion yuan by listing publicly.

The sharp observation about that listing was not about the numbers but the logic: at those profit levels the company does not obviously need outside money, because adding meaningful new capacity costs roughly $7–8 billion and it is generating far more than that. It is listing into high prices, not because it is short of cash. That is usually a signal about where the owners think prices are heading.

Meanwhile OpenAI unveiled its own custom chip, internally called Jalapeño, measured at up to twice the performance per watt of Nvidia's newest platform — and it achieved that without even switching on one of its own speed-boosting features, while Nvidia's result had the equivalent feature enabled. The advantage held across the whole range tested, not at one convenient point.

Putting the three together

Performance per watt simply means how much computing work you get for each unit of electricity. When power is the thing you cannot get more of, this becomes the only efficiency number that matters, because it decides how much computing you can fit behind a fixed electricity connection.

So the chain runs like this. Memory gets expensive, so Nvidia raises prices. Higher prices stretch out how long a data centre takes to pay for itself. Longer payback makes lenders nervous, which raises borrowing costs. And a customer has just proved it can build a chip that does more work per unit of electricity — which is precisely the escape route those customers were already digging.

Passing on a cost increase is a reasonable business decision on its own. Doing it in the same month a major customer demonstrates better silicon is how pricing power turns into customers leaving. Nvidia's share of the value in AI is likely to peak well before its revenue does. That is a problem for the valuation more than for the earnings — and valuations move faster than earnings.

One number that changes every demand forecast

Almost all AI demand forecasts are built on how many people will use these tools and how often. That framing is already out of date. Autonomous software agents — programs that use AI on their own without a person watching — now consume roughly five times the computing that people do, and their usage has grown around fourteen-fold in six months. Humans have become the minority user.

The gap between leading and ordinary corporate users is also widening rather than closing. Across all industries there is roughly an eight-fold difference in AI usage between the typical company and the top tenth. Within technology specifically that gap approaches twelve-fold, and the leading firms are using more than thirty times what they were a little over a year ago. Adoption is not spreading evenly. It is concentrating.

03

The US bond market is being propped up, and it is not working

A government that announces three escalating rescues of its own borrowing costs in a single week, and fails each time, is telling you something about who is left to buy.

Some context first, because this is the part most people skip. When a government spends more than it collects in taxes, it borrows by selling bonds. A bond is an IOU: you lend the government money, it pays you interest, and returns the amount at the end. The interest rate it has to offer is called the yield, and it is set by demand. Plenty of buyers means the government can offer less. Few buyers means it must offer more.

The yield on the ten-year US government bond climbed to its highest in twenty months. That matters far beyond Washington, because that rate is the reference point for mortgages, corporate borrowing, and how every other asset in the world gets valued.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent responded in three escalating steps. First, doubling the size of the Treasury's bond buyback operations from $2 billion to $4 billion — buying back its own older bonds to push prices up and yields down. Then indicating those buybacks could "more than double" again. Then floating the use of the Treasury's roughly $950 billion operating cash account to fund the purchases. Reports described a willingness to do whatever it takes, including selling short-dated debt to buy long-dated debt.

Yields stayed above where they were before the first announcement.

The most striking objection came from Stanley Druckenmiller — one of the most respected investors alive, and Bessent's own former boss for roughly two decades. He went to the Wall Street Journal to say his former colleague was making a mistake by intervening at all. That article then produced a second, smaller controversy when Druckenmiller confirmed he had used AI to help write it and declined to be embarrassed about it.

The problem is specifically at the long end

Here is a detail that gets lost in the headlines and completely changes the diagnosis. Foreign demand for US government debt is the weakest in over a decade — but not uniformly. A two-year bond auction attracted the strongest foreign participation since early 2025. Short-dated debt is selling comfortably.

So buyers are perfectly willing to lend to the US government for two years. They are unwilling to lend for thirty at the interest rate currently on offer. That is not a crisis of confidence in America. It is a judgement about the next few decades, and the arithmetic supports it.

Federal debt held by the public has reached 100% of GDP — the highest since the Second World War. More useful than the level is the forecasting record. The official 2009 projection said the ratio would reach 42% by 2019. It actually reached 78%, an overshoot of thirty-six percentage points. Current official projections show 120% by 2035. Apply the historical pattern of underestimation and you get closer to 135%.

That first number is the one to sit with. For two full years, Americans have spent faster than their real incomes grew. The gap has to come from somewhere, and it has come from savings and borrowing — credit card balances rose $21 billion in a single quarter to $1.26 trillion, the second-highest ever. The previous record run was about twenty-three months, in the late 1970s. It did not end gently.

The third number explains a lot of investor behaviour. For fifty years, roughly one in five large American companies paid a dividend better than the government bond, so income investors had a genuine choice. Now it is one in thirty. If you want income, the bond is the obvious answer, which is part of why money has been leaving shares — and why the government's difficulty in placing long-dated bonds matters even to equity investors.

Two housing markets in one country

The strangest data of all concerns US housing, where two credible measurements pointed in opposite directions simultaneously.

On one side, an estimated 51% more sellers than buyers — the second-highest reading ever. Active buyers at the lowest number on record. Roughly 80% of major cities are now buyer's markets, with sellers outnumbering buyers by 154% in Miami. New home sales collapsed and consumer confidence hit a seven-month low.

On the other, the main US house price index came in above expectations and accelerating, with prices rising at their fastest pace in a year and San Francisco leading — attributed directly to AI wealth.

How both can be true

The resolution sits in one detail: the median price of a new home fell to a five-year low while the average price barely moved. Median means the middle house. Average is dragged around by extremes. When the middle falls and the average holds, a small number of very expensive sales are carrying the whole figure.

So this is not one housing market behaving oddly. It is two markets stacked on top of each other — a small group made suddenly wealthy by AI shares buying expensive homes in a handful of cities, sitting above an ordinary household that has run down its savings for two years and can no longer stretch to a mortgage. National averages hide this completely. It is a reminder that whenever a headline number contradicts what you observe on the ground, the distribution is usually where the answer is.

The oil price is telling you the wrong story

Crude fell steadily. Almost every other measure of what it costs to physically move goods went up. That gap is where the money is.

The headline sequence looked good for anyone who buys fuel. The US Treasury launched a campaign to cut Iran out of the dollar system entirely, warning that any country or bank helping it would lose access. President Trump then announced that all mines had been cleared from the Strait of Hormuz with a zero-tolerance policy on new ones. Oman's foreign minister said a temporary shipping corridor would be announced shortly. Brent crude fell through $90.

Some background on why the Strait of Hormuz matters so much: it is a narrow stretch of water between Iran and Oman, and roughly a fifth of the world's oil passes through it. At its narrowest the shipping lanes are only a couple of miles wide. There is no alternative route for most of that oil. That is why a disruption there moves prices worldwide and why mines in that water are a global economic event, not a regional one.

How to settle it yourselfBoth figures can be arithmetically correct at the same time. Traffic can rise 400% from a collapsed base and still sit 75% below normal — that is just what percentages do when you start from a low number. Counting ships will not resolve this. Two things will: whether a government-backed war risk insurance scheme is still thought necessary, and whether that "temporary" corridor becomes permanent. Insurance is the honest signal, because nobody underwrites a route that has genuinely reopened.

Who pays and who collects

The freight shock is not abstract. Indian tyre makers are expected to see operating margins fall by 200 to 250 basis points — that is 2 to 2.5 percentage points, taking them from about 14.2% down to around 12% — driven by a 35 to 40% rise in raw material costs traceable to the conflict and to tight natural rubber supply. A large Indian textile exporter reported container shortages from the same cause.

V R Sharma, managing director of Jindal Steel, named freight as the single biggest disadvantage Indian steel faces globally — and made a structural point that deserves more attention than it got: China does not suffer the same problem because it owns a large shipping fleet. India does not. When freight rates spike, a Chinese exporter is largely paying itself, while an Indian exporter is paying a foreign shipowner. He also proposed two specific fixes: a high-speed freight corridor through Bangladesh connecting West Bengal to the north-eastern states, and a 50% railway freight subsidy for goods heading there.

On the other side of the same event, a specific set of businesses collects. Damage to oil infrastructure combined with shipping risk pushes volume from tankers toward pipelines, which raises demand for large-diameter welded pipe. Gas infrastructure being built out in Saudi Arabia and the UAE cascades further into cryogenic tanks, pressure vessels, gas cylinders, heat exchangers and vaporisers — and into terminal storage and freight logistics.

The clean way to hold this

Treat it as a transfer rather than a loss. Cost is moving from companies that ship goods to companies that own the infrastructure for moving them a different way. Exporters locked into fixed-price contracts with long delivery times absorb it. Pipe, storage, terminal and shipping owners collect it.

The risk in the second half of that trade is symmetrical and easy to forget: if the corridor genuinely reopens, the infrastructure names give back their gains much faster than the squeezed exporters recover their margins. Pipeline orders are announced in days; margin recovery takes several quarters of renegotiated contracts.

05

India: a very loud government and a very quiet stock market

Policy is moving faster than it has in years. The index has not made a new high in nearly two years. Those two facts have one explanation, and it is arithmetic rather than mood.

What the government actually decided

Defence Minister Rajnath Singh approved transferring the Defence Research and Development Organisation's technology for all conventional missile systems to Indian private companies. To understand why this is significant: for decades, missiles in India were effectively made by one state-owned company, Bharat Dynamics. Private firms supplied parts but did not build complete systems. That arrangement has now ended for conventional weapons. Nuclear and strategic missiles remain entirely state-controlled. Tactical missile work is being distributed among four private prime contractors, including Adani Defence, Bharat Forge, ICOMM and Solar Defence.

Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal committed to amending regulations within two months to remove obstacles for semiconductor and auto-component companies, with a second phase of the semiconductor programme approved and an ambition to seed around $50 billion into the sector over eighteen months. He then led the largest business delegation India has ever sent abroad — over 200 people across Tokyo, Nagoya and Osaka — including a roundtable with Keidanren, the Japanese business federation representing more than 1,500 major companies. Japan is targeting ¥10 trillion of investment into India and is already its fifth-largest foreign investor.

A ₹62,500 crore mobile phone manufacturing scheme was notified. Within a day, Apple signalled expanding local manufacturing beyond iPhones into iPads and Macs, and Google signalled moving Pixel phone, watch and earbud production toward India and Vietnam by 2027, working with Dixon Technologies.

So why has none of this moved the market?

Over the past year, Korea's index rose 113%, Taiwan 85%, Japan 54%, Singapore 36%, Thailand 31%, the Nasdaq 21%. India's Nifty fell 3% and the Sensex fell 5%. Indian shares are at their weakest against US shares in years, and it has been roughly 697 days since the market last touched a high.

The explanation is supply of shares, and it is measurable rather than psychological.

Foreign investors put ₹23,544 crore into Indian equities in one month. Domestic monthly savings plans — where ordinary Indians automatically invest a fixed amount each month — are running near ₹31,961 crore. That is a lot of buying.

Against it, Indian companies and their existing large shareholders raised over ₹1.11 lakh crore in two months through new listings, institutional placements and block sales — the highest such stretch since late 2024.

Individual transactions make the point better than the total. In a single day, SoftBank sold ₹2,884 crore of Lenskart shares, and Y Combinator entities sold ₹970 crore of Meesho. On the other side of both trades: Kotak, ICICI, SBI, Motilal Oswal, Canara Robeco, Franklin, HSBC, Sundaram and several insurers — almost entirely Indian institutions.

What is really happening

Money is arriving in India. It is just not arriving to people who already own shares. It is being met, almost rupee for rupee, by newly created shares and by early investors turning positions into cash at prices they choose.

That is the mechanical reason excellent policy news and a flat index can coexist for two years, and it will continue for exactly as long as the supply of new shares matches the inflow.

The structural fact underneath matters more than the cyclical one. The main buyer of Indian shares is now an ordinary household's monthly instalment. The main seller is a foreign fund exiting on its own schedule. That arrangement has never been tested through a long, painful fall. Watch the calendar of new listings and share sales with the same attention you give the news — if issuance slows while inflows hold, the index can move a great deal without any new good news at all.

Two pieces of history make the caution concrete. A study of 377 Indian listings between 2005 and 2015 found only about 10% beat the market over the following three years. Among recent listings: FirstCry down roughly 58% from its offer price, Ola Electric down 50%, Swiggy down 28%, Cello World down 44%, Vikram Solar down 48%, Oswal Pumps down 51%. Separately, profitability data shows cement, consumer durables, energy, IT services and chemicals all sitting well below their own historical ranges — the index is flat while a good part of the real economy is running below its normal.

The refinancing almost nobody wrote about

The most consequential Indian development got a fraction of the attention given to smaller news.

The Reserve Bank of India ran a special foreign exchange swap window that has raised $72.85 billion against a target of at least $80 billion. The bulk — $65.40 billion — came through foreign currency deposits from non-resident Indians, with the rest from overseas borrowings. Momentum was extraordinary: $16 billion arrived in eight days. The deposit window was closed a month early because the response was so strong.

Here is why it matters, in plain terms. Indian banks compete fiercely for deposits, and that competition pushes up what they pay savers, which squeezes their profits. Bulk deposits domestically cost banks 7 to 8%. These foreign currency deposits cost 6 to 7%. That one percentage point is a direct saving on a very large pool of money. Banks rushed offshore before the deadline — ICICI Bank doubled its overseas borrowing limit to $5 billion, HDFC Bank raised $1.75 billion, with State Bank of India, Axis, IDFC First and Kotak Mahindra all tapping the market.

Why this is the most underrated news in the whole picture

This is two things at once: a defence of the rupee, and a cheaper funding base for the banking system, delivered through a single instrument.

The scale is what makes it serious. In the previous financial year the RBI had to inject roughly ₹6 lakh crore into the system through open market operations to keep enough rupees circulating. Officials indicated those measures will not be needed this year.

India has effectively refinanced part of its banking system's deposit cost through money raised abroad, and simultaneously reduced its dependence on expensive domestic liquidity support. The practical consequence is that the deposit war squeezing Indian bank margins should ease for several quarters. Most emerging economies are doing the opposite right now.

Two supporting signals sit alongside it. India's 28 Regional Rural Banks — small banks serving villages and small towns — posted their highest ever combined profit of ₹10,176 crore, up 49%, with bad loans falling to record lows of 5.3% gross and 2.1% net. And REC, a state-owned power financier, is preparing India's first tokenised corporate bond: under ₹5 billion, settled using the RBI's wholesale digital rupee, with a secondary market targeted by December. Tokenised simply means the bond is recorded on a blockchain so it can settle instantly instead of over days. That places India alongside Europe and Hong Kong, and it is an actual issue rather than an announcement.

The most contrarian claim worth taking seriously

Amid widespread excitement about an Indian capital spending revival, one analysis argued the opposite: the recent quarter was not the arrival of a capex cycle. The money was already spent, across the previous three financial years. This is simply the year that factories built earlier start running closer to full capacity.

The implication is uncomfortable. For most companies currently valued on a capex recovery, the earnings two years out have already been paid for. The spending that produces them is behind, not ahead. There is no second wave coming unless someone commits new money.

The same work identified what actually separated companies in the period, and neither factor was demand. First, a cost shock — cooking gas moving from ₹60 to ₹200 per kilogram, container costs tripling — which hit everyone at once. Second, Europe: three European driveline component manufacturers went insolvent within six months. "Europe plus one" is quietly a bigger story than "China plus one" — but it cuts both ways, because a European customer cutting production is lost revenue arriving now, while replacement business arrives over years and needs approval first.

06

Where India's manufacturing money actually ends up

Four cases where the instinctive answer and the careful answer point in different directions.

Opening up missiles moves value rather than creating it

Almost everyone read the missile decision as "more missiles get built, the sector wins." One dissenting reading was the correct one: it is negative for Bharat Dynamics, which loses a monopoly and will face margin pressure, and positive for Bharat Electronics, which can now enter the missile domain through partnerships with private firms.

The general rule is worth keeping. Breaking a monopoly does not create value — it relocates it. Value moves away from the protected assembler and toward whatever stays scarce. In a missile, the scarce things are propellants and explosives, the seeker that guides it to its target, precision forgings, and — least discussed, most reliable — testing and certification capacity, because every new private production line must be approved before it can ship a single unit.

There is also a timing trap. Approval, certification and technology transfer frameworks take years. None of this appears in next year's profits. It will trade on order announcements rather than results, which means positioning is driven by news rather than earnings — a pattern with a poor record for whoever arrives last.

Getting the drawings is not the same as getting the technology

The single most useful piece of thinking in this whole picture concerned a proposed transfer of small satellite launch vehicle technology from ISRO to Hindustan Aeronautics Limited: ₹511 crore, two years working alongside ISRO staff, two rockets, and then a non-exclusive licence with no continuing obligation on ISRO.

The critique was precise. ISRO itself took over a decade, three development flights and one failure to make that rocket work. Drawings are perhaps a tenth of what a rocket programme actually runs on. The other nine-tenths is unwritten knowledge — why a particular weld goes in at that exact angle, which faint vibration during a test means stop everything. That knowledge lives inside the engineers who were there, and it moves only by working next to them.

The comparison offered was exact and worth remembering. The Su-30MKI fighter programme delivered 222 aircraft over sixteen years, at roughly ₹100 crore more per aircraft than simply importing them, and reached 13% Indian content against 60% promised — because Russia sent assembly kits but kept the design work. Against that, the Dhruv helicopter programme begun in 1984 used the German firm MBB as a genuine design partner, with Indian and German engineers working together on rotor dynamics. HAL's design division can now produce original designs of its own.

The rule you can carry anywhere

This applies far beyond defence — to semiconductors, electronics assembly, pharmaceuticals, anything.

The test of a technology partnership is not what gets licensed. It is whether engineers from both sides do design work together, over a long period, on the same problem. A licence gives you the ability to assemble. Joint design gives you the ability to design.

When any Indian company announces a foreign technology tie-up, ask which of the two it actually is. Most announcements are the first while being described as the second, and the difference only becomes visible five years later.

In space, the value is not where the attention is

Two figures reframe the Indian space investment case completely.

The first is a genuine surprise: India pays roughly $13,302 to put one kilogram into orbit, against $3,225 for the United States — four times more expensive. This directly contradicts the widely held belief that India is the world's cheap launch provider. That reputation came from a handful of famously frugal missions, not from routine cost per kilogram.

The second is how the money splits across the industry.

Put the two together

The popular Indian space story inverts. The segment that gets the most attention and national pride — launch — is both the smallest revenue pool in the industry and the one where India is currently expensive rather than cheap.

The listed opportunity sits in precision propulsion components, satellite avionics, radar and ground stations, space-grade radio systems, and launch vehicle structures — companies like MTAR Technologies, Data Patterns and Astra Microwave. The genuinely large long-term pool is downstream analytics, turning raw satellite data into recurring software revenue, and almost all of that is still privately held.

India has 42 gigawatts of clean power nobody wants to buy

India has nearly 42 gigawatts of renewable capacity that has been auctioned but has found no buyer. Roughly 17 to 18 gigawatts is plain solar; another 14 to 15 was awarded at prices too high to sell on.

The reason is structural, not commercial. State electricity distribution companies increasingly need power after dark, and plain solar produces a flood of electricity in the middle of the day and nothing in the evening. About 11% of solar generation was thrown away during the hottest months because the grid could not use it. The transmission network could not absorb more than 8 billion units over three months even as 63 billion units reached it. Nearly 21 gigawatts of projects have only part-time grid access.

The government's response is to change the shape of the supply within already-agreed prices — mainly by adding battery storage so the power can be delivered in the evening — with new rules allowing developers of genuinely unviable projects to walk away without penalty. Officials were candid that a lot of plain solar bids will simply never be sold.

The connection nobody drew

Nobody linked this to the data centre story, and the fit is exact.

Unsold Indian solar exists because distribution companies do not want daytime-only power. A data centre is the one large customer that runs continuously, pairs economically with batteries, and is actively looking for locations because Western grids have run out of room. Andhra Pradesh has already approved a 512 megawatt integrated green data centre at ₹31,387 crore.

The bottleneck for India is not generation — it is transmission and storage. Which is why an ₹85,000 crore transmission order book at Adani Energy Solutions, and a ₹4,700 crore award to carry 4,500 megawatts of renewables and storage from Karnataka into Maharashtra, are the more relevant exposure than the solar developers themselves.

How Indians are spending, and who is capturing it

Three things are happening at once: spending is moving to smaller cities, delivery apps are quietly turning into advertising businesses, and food prices are now set by government notifications rather than by harvests.

The centre of gravity has left the big cities

Smaller Indian cities now account for an estimated 53% of festive-season temporary hiring, up from 42% in 2022, while the big metros have fallen to about 47%. Temporary hiring demand in tier two and tier three cities is growing at 20 to 25% a year, roughly two and a half times the metro rate. Wages in those cities have risen 8 to 10% a year since 2022 against 5 to 6% in metros.

Companies are following. Godrej Enterprises increased festive seasonal budgets 30% with a focus on smaller towns. Haier India is expanding its non-metro frontline workforce 15%. Large electronics chains including Reliance Digital and Croma opened 89 stores in a single month, mostly in emerging hubs like Jaipur, Lucknow, Coimbatore, Bhubaneswar, Indore and Surat.

At the same time, housing sales fell 6% to 91,729 units, dropping in six of eight cities — Pune down 21%, Ahmedabad down 20%, Bengaluru down 9%, Mumbai region down 7%.

Reading the two together

Strong consumption and weak property prices are appearing in the same economy at the same time, separated by geography. Smaller cities are gaining income and jobs. Larger cities are seeing property demand soften.

Supply chains are following the income rather than the property. Dark stores and warehouses are being placed directly inside the towns where people are spending, rather than being supplied from a metro hub. For anyone assessing an Indian consumer company, how deep its distribution reaches into smaller towns has become a more useful number than how its metro stores are performing.

Delivery apps stopped being delivery businesses

Own-brand products — where the platform sells goods under its own label rather than a manufacturer's — have gone from 6 to 8% of Indian quick commerce sales to 12 to 16%. The expansion is now moving into fresh fruit and vegetables, which is the hardest category to run and the most profitable.

Why fresh produce is the prize
CategoryOwn-brand marginWhy
Fresh fruit & vegetables35–45%No brand sits in between. Quality varies by soil and weather so nothing is standardised, and produce sold online is exempt from printing expiry dates — which makes perceived freshness, not a label, the differentiator.
Premium packaged goods20–25%Higher value per item, but competing directly against established brands with real customer loyalty.
Staples (rice, flour, pulses)15–25%Where own-brands started. Easy to copy, price-sensitive, low differentiation.

The operating detail is what makes it work. Zepto's fresh programme, called Bloom, sources directly from more than 1,000 growers across eight states, supporting them on seeds, crop quality, collection, wastage and financing through non-bank lenders. Swiggy Instamart's equivalent, Nectr, was piloted across five dark stores in Bengaluru: fruit and vegetable spending rose 10%, repeat purchase rates rose 12 percentage points, and overall platform retention improved 7 percentage points. Fresh produce already accounts for more than half of Zepto's orders and nearly a third of its revenue.

But the deeper shift is not about groceries at all. The framing that explains it best: the dark store is not the asset — it is the antenna. What it really produces is a signal of what a customer wants at the exact moment they are ready to buy, and that signal is worth far more sold as advertising than as delivery.

The precedent is exact. Amazon discovered around 2012 that the attention it had gathered was worth more than the commerce it enabled, and built what is now roughly a $70 billion annual advertising business at margins above 50%. Jeff Bezos did not plan it that way — he built a marketplace, then Prime, then logistics, and only then noticed what he was actually sitting on. In India the same thing is under way: advertising revenue on Blinkit, Zepto and Instamart is heading toward roughly ₹4,900 crore, with the wider retail media market around ₹6,000 crore.

The loop this creates

Follow it round. Brands hand platforms 30 to 40% of revenue. Platforms use that money to build dark stores. Dark stores generate data on exactly what people buy and when. That data becomes an advertising product. Brands then pay again to reach their own customers through it.

Own-brand fresh produce is the sharpest version, because it is the one category where the platform controls sourcing, quality and price with no brand in between at all.

The sensible counter-move is visible: Varun Beverages, which has spent decades bottling PepsiCo drinks, set up its own ready-to-drink alcohol business called KIVA Spirits and hired Prathmesh Mishra, formerly of Diageo and Pernod Ricard, to run it. Owning a category the platform cannot easily copy beats fighting for shelf space inside someone else's antenna. Expect more established consumer companies to buy into new categories rather than defend old ones.

Food prices are now a policy variable

The sugar sequence is the clearest case study available anywhere.

StageWhat the government didWhy

The same period saw milk price increases, basmati rice climbing, a four-year wheat export ban lifted, and the food safety regulator FSSAI issue 150 notices to Nestlé India, PepsiCo, Coca-Cola India and Mondelez over misleading advertisements and labelling.

One comparison captured the underlying consumer reality better than any price chart. The same soft drink in a can: 61 calories in Vietnam at the equivalent of ₹219, against 168 calories in India at ₹40. Three times the sugar at a fifth of the price. An Indian consumer can buy an expensive imported can or accept the sugar.

What to take from this

Six months from "export the surplus" to "import duty-free" is not a commodity cycle. It is policy whiplash. It means the largest single variable in an Indian agricultural business right now is a government notification, not a monsoon — which makes the commodity itself very hard to have a view on in either direction.

What is possible to have a view on is the second-order effect. Every tightening cycle permanently raises the compliance burden, and compliance spending does not reverse when prices normalise. The durable earner in a regulatory crackdown is the auditor, not the producer — here, food testing and certification companies like Vimta Labs, Choksi Labs and Qualitech Labs. The same pattern re-rated shipping and maritime services after their own rules changed.

When a good story runs a long way ahead of the numbers

Three of the most careful analysts in Indian markets independently attacked the same story within days of each other, without coordinating. That convergence is itself a signal.

The story is "physical AI" — the idea that robotics and machines with built-in intelligence are the next multi-trillion-dollar opportunity, and that certain Indian precision engineering companies are positioned to capture it.

The headline forecast in circulation, from Morgan Stanley, puts the global humanoid robot market above $5 trillion by 2050, with more than a billion robots in use. That figure includes not just robot sales but the entire supply chain, repair and maintenance ecosystem around them.

Now the actual position of the most-discussed Indian candidate, Sona BLW Precision Forgings.

The story versus the numbers
The storyThe position today
A $5 trillion market by 2050Robotics is about 1% of revenue
India positioned across the value chainThe robotics order book is ₹800 crore — 3.3% of a ₹240 billion total order book
A major new business lineThe division was formally created only in July 2026
Broad customer tractionThree orders: one robotic subsystem, one perception engineering contract, one radar perception system for an Indian truck maker
Ten-times revenue growth over a decadeWorking the arithmetic backwards gives roughly 12 to 17% annual returns from here — if everything goes to plan

The company's own framing is honest enough: three revenue streams, starting with components closest to existing capability — motors, gearboxes, sensors — then a perception software layer, then, most ambitiously, building complete robot platforms.

The disciplined way to hold this was set out clearly by one analyst: separate three questions that usually get mashed together. Is the market opportunity real? Yes, the trends support it. Does the company have the capability? Partly — genuine strength in hardware, motors and sensing; software and AI still developing. Is the current price already assuming success? That is the question worth obsessing over.

A second critique was blunter: do not embrace a story when the actual economics — gross margins, how quickly cash comes back, what it does to the balance sheet — are still unclear. Real traction in physical AI probably arrives after 2030, more likely 2035.

A third used history instead of analysis. What happened to all the companies guiding for 25% annual growth three years ago? Fewer than one in ten achieved it. They gave that guidance at fifty times earnings, were promoted as part of a megatrend, and are simply not discussed any more.

A live example landed in the middle of the argument

While that debate was running, MV Electrosystems — a newly listed railway propulsion company — reported results. Revenue of ₹12.77 crore, down 5% on the year and down 14% on the previous quarter. An operating loss of ₹5.37 crore, worse than both comparisons. A net loss of ₹6.89 crore.

This is a company that had been written up on the strength of a ₹921 crore order book covering 572 propulsion systems, against a market value near ₹1,698 crore — with the explicit acknowledgement at the time that you were not paying for current earnings but for what the business might become in three or four years, and that normal valuation measures did not apply. The reaction to the results was a question about whether the stock would open 10% or 20% down.

The distinction all three were circling

The enabler is real. The platform is speculative.

Precision motors, gearboxes and sensors genuinely go into robots, and making them is an extension of what these companies already do well. Building complete robot platforms is an ambition currently supported by three orders.

The same pattern showed up in a completely different industry. In cooling: KRN Heat Exchangers grew revenue 119%, Amber Enterprises reached a 10.8% margin in electronics as its mix moved toward higher-value work, while Blue Star's consumer air-conditioner margin fell from 5.8% to 2.9% defending market share in a weak summer. The money is behind the box, not in the box.

Two ideas about thinking, worth separating out

The first is the most useful thing here for anyone who reads a lot. Collecting information is not the same as building conviction. You read one positive article, then another, then hear management explain the opportunity, then see someone repeat the view, then a brokerage report gives a similar target. The idea feels stronger each time. But the evidence did not get stronger — the same idea simply arrived in different packaging. Repetition creates familiarity, familiarity creates comfort, and comfort is easily mistaken for conviction. Real conviction comes from taking your key assumptions and actively trying to break them. If the idea survives, your conviction is earned. If it does not, you avoided a mistake. Either way the research did its job. Conviction is not built by finding more reasons to invest; it is built by eliminating reasons not to.

The second concerns cyclical industries, using chemicals as the example. Chemical shares do not move with earnings — they move with expectations. Peak earnings arrive together with peak optimism: record margins, confident management, rising estimates, new capacity announced across the industry. At that exact point the market has already priced those conditions and starts looking at what could weaken next. By the time earnings genuinely fall, the share price has often already corrected. The reverse holds at the bottom, where weak demand and squeezed margins make valuations look expensive precisely because earnings have collapsed — which is when the best opportunities appear.

Both ideas were live simultaneously. H-acid, a dye intermediate, rose 28% in a month after Chinese plants closed and one large producer took a maintenance shutdown. Maleic anhydride rose 93% in two months. Kiri Industries, Bodal Chemicals, Bhageria Industries and IG Petrochemicals all moved immediately — well before any of it could appear in a reported result.

09

China is compounding, Europe is shrinking

The most under-discussed imbalance in the whole picture, and it lands directly on Indian exporters from both sides.

What China demonstrated

A humanoid robot named Lightning, built by Honor, ran 100 metres in 9.32 seconds at a robotics competition in Beijing — faster than Usain Bolt's world record — and broke the 400 metre record at the same event. In a Beijing shopping mall it is now unremarkable to pay 89 yuan for ten minutes playing with a robot dog. Haier displayed the world's first home cooking robot alongside a domestic service robot, with the strategic logic being that a robot built to work with that company's existing appliances is how an appliance maker locks a household into its whole system.

Landspace, a private launch company, successfully recovered a first-stage rocket booster and published an unusually candid account of what comes next: return it to the factory for inspection and partial disassembly, with the genuinely hard problem being actual reuse rather than recovery. It faces intense competition on cost from both the state programme and other private firms — which is the setup for a pattern China has already run through electric vehicles, solar, wind, AI and robotics. There is even a Chinese word for it: involution, meaning brutal internal competition that crushes margins for everyone while making the product dramatically cheaper for the rest of the world.

Electric and hybrid vehicles reached 65% of Chinese car sales in a single month, measured by actual registrations. At the same time Li Auto cut prices on a model one month after launching it because orders were not matching expectations — competition in large SUVs is now universal. Chinese open-weight language models reached parity with leading closed models on real software tasks at a fraction of the cost, prompting the observation that this is excellent for spreading AI around and for small companies, and genuinely bad for the incumbents charging for it.

Militarily, a Chinese J-16 fighter made its first overseas deployment — over 6,000 kilometres with a single stop in Pakistan — to exercise against Egypt's French-built Rafales. The strategic reading offered was that this demonstrates to an entire region that China can replace the West as both technology and security supplier, including integrated air defence, satellites, signals intelligence and electronic warfare, while simultaneously making large industrial investments the West is not making in a country that needs them.

What Europe demonstrated

Volkswagen is set to announce 50,000 job cuts, with its union saying 140,000 jobs are at risk and describing the turnaround plan as detached from reality. Three European driveline component manufacturers entered insolvency within six months. Braskem, a large Brazilian petrochemical group, filed for out-of-court restructuring.

Trade escalated on two fronts at once. US tariffs on Canadian goods went to 50% on roughly $20 billion of products, with Canada retaliating from early September including doubling its own steel tariffs to 50%, and a further increase on Canadian cars, trucks, parts and steel to 50% scheduled for 1 January 2027. Trump's framing was that "Canada will be treated like a state no longer." A further 7.5% tariff on Chinese goods was floated, taking the total to 20%, timed to land before a leaders' summit.

Who actually holds the leverage between the US and Canada?
The obvious answer

The US is the bigger economy and the bigger customer. Canada absorbs the tariffs or concedes. Most commentary stopped here.

The less obvious one

There is a recurring pattern of overestimating leverage. In the middle of a strained bond market, a looming energy crunch and approaching midterm elections, this may be the strongest hand Canada will ever hold — because it supplies resources everyone currently wants, into a market with a genuine supply problem in energy and metals. And if Canada is forced to sell US government bonds to defend its own currency, that creates a separate and larger problem for Washington.

Why this matters well beyond CanadaThe second reading connects two stories that are normally kept in separate boxes — trade policy and the bond market. Any large foreign holder of US government debt that comes under currency pressure becomes a forced seller into a market already struggling to place long-dated bonds. Trade escalation and the yield problem are not independent of each other, and a policy that looks costless in trade terms may not be costless in financing terms.
What this means for Indian exporters

European industrial contraction is being read in India as an opportunity — capacity leaving, market share available. In the near term it is the opposite. A European customer cutting production is revenue lost this quarter, while replacement business arrives over years and needs qualification first. Companies with meaningful European exposure should be expected to report the pain first and the opportunity much later.

Meanwhile Chinese competitive intensity is being exported into every category India is trying to enter, at a cost structure India cannot currently match. The shipping fleet gap makes it worse, because Chinese exporters are insulated from exactly the freight shock Indian exporters are absorbing. India is squeezed from both directions at once.

Arguments worth keeping open

Where credible people reached opposite conclusions from the same facts. Splitting the difference destroys the information, so here they are properly.

Is the Indian household dangerously over-borrowed?
The concern

Household debt has risen from around 20% of GDP before the pandemic to nearly 46% now, against 55% in the United States. At that level the Indian household looks stretched and could crack.

Income has grown more slowly than consumption since 2020, which points to spending funded by borrowing. Presented as one of three reasons foreign investors are hesitant, alongside AI displacing white-collar jobs and a perceived stall in economic reform.

The challenge

Where does that number come from? It looks well off what the central bank's own data shows. At 46% of GDP it would imply something like ₹120 lakh crore of household debt, which seems too large.

This one can actually be settled — and it has beenThe Reserve Bank of India's own Financial Stability Report puts household debt at 45.5% of GDP as of June 2026, up from roughly 33% in 2018-19. So the concern is essentially accurate and the challenge is not. But the RBI's own analysis adds the detail that matters more than the headline: 58.4% of household borrowing is now non-housing retail credit — personal loans, credit cards, consumer finance — rather than home loans. That is the real issue. A home loan is secured against an asset and builds equity. A personal loan is secured against nothing but future income. Bad loans on unsecured lending run at 1.7% against 0.7% on secured lending, more than double. Gold loans alone reached ₹5.14 trillion. The level is manageable by global standards. The composition is what deserves watching.
Sam Altman admitted AI is arriving slower than he expected. Is that good news or bad news?
Read as a warning

The OpenAI chief executive conceded he expected far more disruption far sooner after GPT-4 in 2023 — software businesses up for grabs immediately — and was wrong about the speed, because "the economy just has so much inertia." People keep doing the same things and buying from the same companies. Read this way, it is a walkback of the entire near-term revenue case.

Read as an opportunity

Slower adoption means a longer period during which anyone genuinely skilled with these tools has an advantage over everyone who is not. The rest of the economy fights inertia at a glacial pace while a small group compounds ahead. That advantage only closes when the tools become good enough and easy enough that skill stops mattering.

Both are right, about different questionsSlow adoption is bad news if you bought AI exposure expecting revenue next year. It is good news if your advantage comes from being early yourself. Which applies to you depends entirely on whether you are buying the trade or living inside it — and most people never separate those two positions clearly enough to notice they are holding both.
Should the US Treasury be intervening in its own bond market?
Do not fight it

Buybacks doubled, possibly to double again, with a $950 billion cash account available and the possible elimination of long-dated issuance. The stated position is whatever it takes. The Federal Reserve cannot cut interest rates in this inflation environment, so direct intervention is the only lever left.

This is the mistake

Raised by the most senior possible source — Bessent's own employer for roughly two decades. Wading into the bond market is the error, not the fix.

One historical pattern to weigh it againstBoth positions come from people with real standing and they cannot both be right. The regularity worth carrying: interventions that work tend to be announced once and believed. Interventions requiring three escalating announcements in a single week, with yields still above where they started, are usually losing. That is an observation about market psychology rather than a forecast — but it is the kind of observation that has been right more often than not.
Does owning both a hospital and a health insurer solve the conflict of interest, or hide it?
Narayana Health's position

Seeing the patient across clinics, diagnostics, primary care and treatment creates an information advantage that can improve risk assessment across the whole insurance book. The combined structure acts as a self-check: too much treatment makes the insurance business unsustainable, too little damages patient outcomes.

The investor's response

A structure is not automatically a mechanism. Does the insurance arm genuinely challenge unnecessary treatment, and does the hospital respond? Or does the group simply get better at moving profit between two pockets? Treat it as something to be tested, not as proof. And note that as the insurance book grows, so does the temptation to shift economics internally.

The idea generalises well beyond hospitalsIf you own a business, ask where the profit comes from. Treating more patients efficiently is good business. Building capacity that was needed is what capital is for. But if profit increasingly depends on the customer being unable to judge whether the service was necessary, you own something different from what you think you own. And one discipline worth adopting everywhere: before buying anything, ask what evidence would change your mind. If nothing could, you are not monitoring a business — you are defending a position.

Words explained

Plain meanings for every term used above, so nothing here needs a second tab.

Balance sheetA list of everything a company owns and everything it owes, at one point in time.
Off-balance-sheet debtMoney owed that does not appear on that list, usually because a separate legal company technically borrowed it.
ConsolidationCombining a subsidiary's assets and debts into the parent's accounts. Avoiding it keeps the debt invisible at the parent level.
Finance vs operating leaseA finance lease is treated like buying something with borrowed money and shows as capital spending. An operating lease is treated like ordinary renting and often does not.
Credit default swapInsurance against a borrower failing to repay. When its price hits records, professional lenders are worried even if the share price is not falling.
YieldThe return a bond pays. When bond prices rise, yields fall, and vice versa — they always move opposite ways.
Bond buybackA government buying back its own older bonds to push their price up and the yield down.
CorrelationWhether two things move together. Plus one is lockstep, zero is unrelated, minus one is exactly opposite.
Basis pointOne hundredth of a percentage point. A 250 basis point fall in margin is a fall of 2.5 percentage points.
Median vs averageMedian is the middle value. Average is pulled around by extremes. When they diverge, the distribution is the story.
GigawattRoughly the output of one large nuclear reactor, or enough electricity for about 750,000 homes.
Demand responseA large electricity user agreeing to cut consumption when the grid is stressed. Only counts as spare capacity if the load can genuinely be interrupted.
CurtailmentElectricity that gets generated and then thrown away because the grid cannot carry or use it.
Performance per wattHow much computing work a chip does per unit of electricity. When power is scarce, this decides how much computing fits behind a fixed connection.
Double orderingPlacing one requirement with several suppliers intending to cancel the losers. It inflates every supplier's reported order book.
Payback periodYears taken to earn back what a project cost. Three years means it funds the next one itself; six means it needs continuous outside money.
Order bookWork won but not yet delivered. Useful for visibility, but only as reliable as the contracts inside it.
Offer for sale / placementTwo ways of selling shares. One sells shares existing holders already own; the other creates new ones. Both add supply to the market.
Secured vs unsecured loanA secured loan has an asset behind it that the lender can take. An unsecured one has only the borrower's future income.
Private labelA retailer's own-brand product. Higher margin for the retailer, and direct competition for the brands selling through it.
Dark storeA small warehouse that looks like a shop but serves only delivery orders. No customers walk in.
Contract manufacturer (CDMO)A company that researches and makes drugs on behalf of the firm whose name appears on the box.
Tokenised bondA bond recorded on a blockchain so ownership can transfer and settle almost instantly instead of over several days.
InvolutionA Chinese term for brutal internal competition that destroys margins domestically while making the product far cheaper for everyone else.
